Working Papers

Marriage Squeeze and Education Choice: Evidence from China's Imbalanced Sex Ratios

Presentations: Canadian Economics Association Annual Conference, Vancouver, 2026.

Abstract

China's male-biased sex ratios transformed a demographic imbalance into marriage-market competition. This paper asks whether exposure to higher male-to-female sex ratios creates incentives to acquire education before marriage. Using 2010 China census microdata for cohorts born between 1975 and 1990, the paper assigns sex ratios at birth measured at the birth-market level. Higher sex-ratio exposure is associated with later first marriage for both men and women, and with a larger delay and a wider spousal age gap for men. Education responses are positive but modest in the pooled sample and mask substantial heterogeneity: they are stronger in urban markets and in East/Northeast and Central provinces. The results remain robust after controlling for One-Child Policy intensity. Although both men and women increase education in response to higher sex ratios, they appear to do so under different incentives: men benefit more through improved matching prospects in a male-surplus market, while women benefit more through match quality and household bargaining position. A DiNardo-Fortin-Lemieux decomposition shows that education upgrading explains a larger share of the overall change in age at first marriage, while sex-ratio change contributes more for men and urban residents. The findings suggest that marriage-market pressure can create an additional incentive for schooling, but only where education is a feasible and valued margin of adjustment.
